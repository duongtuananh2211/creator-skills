# Storytelling frameworks: classic and modern (business/communication)

Note on sourcing: claims marked [BK] are from the researcher's background knowledge and were NOT re-verified with a fetched source in this session; the report writer should treat them as probable but unconfirmed (or drop them). Everything else carries an inline source.

## Key Question 1: Classic frameworks (origin, steps, best use, strengths/limits, example)

### Takeaway
Nearly every classic Western model elaborates Aristotle's "whole with a beginning, middle and end" built on causally linked action and a turning point; they differ in granularity (3 acts -> 5 stages -> 8 steps -> 12 stages -> 15 beats) and in whether they describe drama in general (Aristotle, Freytag, Field), the protagonist's inner/outer transformation (Campbell, Vogler, Harmon), or the emotional trajectory only (Vonnegut, Reagan et al.). Kishōtenketsu is the main non-Western counter-model: it builds meaning through contrast/twist rather than escalating conflict.

### Cited Findings

#### 1. Aristotle, *Poetics* (c. 335 BCE)
- Origin: Aristotle's lecture notes on tragedy (c. 4th century BCE). Defines a plot ("mythos") as a "whole" that "has a beginning, a middle, and an end"; plot is "the soul of tragedy", ranked above character; key devices are reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis); events should follow by "necessity or probability" — [Aristotle, Poetics, trans. S. H. Butcher, MIT Internet Classics Archive](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.html) [BK: page not fetched this session; quotes are the standard Butcher translation]
- Components: beginning (needs nothing before it), middle (follows and is followed), end (follows and nothing after); unity of action; complication (desis) + unravelling (lusis). [BK]
- Best for: diagnosing whether any story is causally coherent; foundation for all later models. Limitation: a theory of Greek tragedy, prescriptive and very abstract — gives no step-by-step template. [Inference]
- Note: The Wikipedia "Three-act structure" article does not attribute the three-act model to Aristotle — the "Aristotle invented 3 acts" claim is a popular simplification — [Wikipedia: Three-act structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-act_structure)

#### 2. Freytag's Pyramid (Gustav Freytag, 1863)
- Origin: *Die Technik des Dramas* (1863), a study of five-act drama (Shakespeare, Greek and German classical drama) — [MasterClass: Freytag's Pyramid](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/freytags-pyramid); [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/freytags-pyramid/)
- Five parts: Exposition -> Rising action -> Climax (turning point / "point of no return") -> Falling action -> Catastrophe (tragedy) or Dénouement/Resolution (comedy). Freytag frames the drama as "play and counterplay" forming a triangle — [Scribophile: Freytag's Pyramid](https://www.scribophile.com/blog/freytags-pyramid/); [TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/freytags-pyramid/)
- Applicability: can be applied (sometimes modified) to short stories and novels; but modern plays can be hard to fit (example given: *Driving Miss Daisy*) — [Scribophile](https://www.scribophile.com/blog/freytags-pyramid/)
- Example: *Romeo and Juliet* — exposition (feud), rising action (secret marriage), climax (Romeo kills Tybalt), falling action (banishment, potion plan), catastrophe (double suicide). [BK]
- Limitation: climax placed at the midpoint reflects 19th-c. five-act tragedy; modern film/novels usually place the climax near the end. [Inference/BK]

#### 3. Three-Act Structure / "Paradigm" (Syd Field, 1979)
- Origin: popularised by Syd Field in *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (1979); Setup / Confrontation / Resolution — [Wikipedia: Three-act structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-act_structure)
- Components: Act I establishes characters & world, inciting incident, first plot point ends Act I and raises the dramatic question; Act II protagonist struggles, situations worsen, character develops; Act III climax answers the dramatic question, subplots resolve — [Wikipedia: Three-act structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-act_structure)
- Field's classic proportions for a ~120-page script: Act I ≈ 30 pp, Act II ≈ 60 pp, Act III ≈ 30 pp (≈1:2:1), Plot Point I ~p.25–27, Plot Point II ~p.85–90; later added "midpoint". [BK]
- Critiques exist even in the reference literature: James Bonnet, "What's Wrong With The Three Act Structure" vs. Yves Lavandier's defence — [Wikipedia: Three-act structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-act_structure)
- Best for: feature films, any medium-length narrative, also presentations (intro/body/close). Strength: simple, universal vocabulary; Limitation: Act II "sagging middle" gives little guidance — which is why later models (Save the Cat, midpoint) subdivide it. [Inference]

#### 4. Monomyth / Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell, 1949) and Vogler's 12 stages (memo mid-1980s; book 1992)
- Campbell: *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949); comparative-mythology claim that myths share a pattern of Departure (Separation) -> Initiation -> Return, in 17 stages (Call to Adventure, Refusal, Supernatural Aid, Crossing the Threshold, Belly of the Whale, Road of Trials, … Return Threshold, Master of Two Worlds, Freedom to Live). Core formula: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder…" [BK — see [Wikipedia: Hero's journey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero%27s_journey), not fetched]
- Vogler: as a Disney story analyst he wrote a 7-page internal memo "A Practical Guide to The Hero with a Thousand Faces" that circulated widely in Hollywood; expanded into *The Writer's Journey: Mythic Structure for Storytellers and Screenwriters* (1992); 2nd ed. 1998 retitled *…Mythic Structure for Writers*; 3rd ed. 2007; 25th-anniversary ed. July 2020 — [Wikipedia: The Writer's Journey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Writer%27s_Journey:_Mythic_Structure_for_Writers); [Wikipedia: Christopher Vogler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Vogler)
- Vogler's 12 stages: 1 Ordinary World; 2 Call to Adventure; 3 Refusal of the Call; 4 Meeting with the Mentor; 5 Crossing the First Threshold; 6 Tests, Allies, Enemies; 7 Approach to the Inmost Cave; 8 The Ordeal (death & rebirth); 9 Reward; 10 The Road Back; 11 Resurrection (final death/rebirth); 12 Return with the Elixir — [Wikipedia: The Writer's Journey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Writer%27s_Journey:_Mythic_Structure_for_Writers)
- Discrepancy: one review lists Vogler's structure as 11 stages — [profitadvisors.com review](https://www.profitadvisors.com/journey.shtml); the 12-stage list in the Wikipedia article is the standard version.
- Memo date: commonly given as 1985 [BK]; NOT confirmed by fetched sources (see Gaps).
- Vogler also described 8 character archetypes (Hero, Mentor, Threshold Guardian, Herald, Shapeshifter, Shadow, Ally, Trickster). [BK]
- Example: *Star Wars* (1977) — Luke on Tatooine (ordinary world), Leia's message (call), refuses Obi-Wan, mentor Obi-Wan, leaves Tatooine, Mos Eisley cantina (tests), Death Star (inmost cave), trash compactor (ordeal), rescue Leia (reward), escape, Death Star trench run (resurrection), medal ceremony (return). Lucas acknowledged Campbell's influence. [BK]
- Best for: epic/adventure/fantasy, transformation arcs, founder/brand origin stories, long-form; strength: rich archetypes and emotional beats; limitation: heavy, easily formulaic (see Key Question 4).

#### 5. Dan Harmon's Story Circle (2000s)
- Origin: TV writer Dan Harmon (*Community*, *Rick and Morty*), simplified Campbell into 8 steps, also called the "plot embryo"/"story embryo" — [Reedsy: Dan Harmon Story Circle](https://reedsy.com/blog/guide/story-structure/dan-harmon-story-circle/); [The Book Designer](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/the-dan-harmon-story-circle/). Harmon developed it around the Channel 101 short-film festival era (early 2000s) and published it on his wiki/blog [BK].
- Steps: 1 YOU (character in comfort zone) -> 2 NEED (wants something) -> 3 GO (enters unfamiliar situation) -> 4 SEARCH (adapts / faces challenges) -> 5 FIND (gets what they wanted) -> 6 TAKE (pays a heavy price) -> 7 RETURN (back to familiar situation) -> 8 CHANGE (having changed) — [Boords: Story Circle](https://boords.com/blog/storytelling-101-the-dan-harmon-story-circle); [The Book Designer](https://www.thebookdesigner.com/the-dan-harmon-story-circle/)
- Presented as descriptive, not prescriptive — [Spines: Dan Harmon Story Circle](https://spines.com/dan-harmon-story-circle/)
- Circle geometry [BK]: top half = order/conscious world, bottom half = chaos/unconscious; steps 1→5 mirror 5→1 (e.g., need ↔ take).
- Best for: episodic TV, short films, YouTube/social video, any short piece that needs a full arc; repeatable per episode/scene. Limitation: still protagonist-centred and change-based; less useful for argument-driven content. [Inference]
- Example (Rick and Morty-style episode): Morty is bored at school (you) → wants Jessica's attention (need) → jumps to an alien world (go) → struggles (search) → gets a love potion (find) → it mutates the world (take) → they flee back (return) → Morty learns consequences (change). [Illustrative, invented]

#### 6. Kurt Vonnegut's "shapes of stories" + Reagan et al. (2016)
- Vonnegut's rejected master's anthropology thesis (University of Chicago) plotted stories on axes "Beginning–End" vs "Ill Fortune–Great Fortune"; he called it his greatest contribution; compared Cinderella to the Old Testament creation/Christian story; said "There is no reason why the simple shapes of stories can't be fed into computers, they are beautiful shapes." — [Reagan et al. 2016, arXiv HTML (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07772)
- Vonnegut's named shapes include "Man in a Hole", "Boy Meets Girl", "Cinderella", "From Bad to Worse" (Kafka), and the ambiguous "Hamlet"; presented in *Palm Sunday* (1981), his lectures, and *A Man Without a Country* (2005). [BK]
- Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth, Dodds, "The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes", *EPJ Data Science* 5:31 (2016); arXiv 1606.07772. Sentiment analysis (hedonometer) of 1,327 Project Gutenberg fiction texts; verified with matrix decomposition (SVD), supervised learning and unsupervised (clustering, self-organising map) — [EPJ Data Science abstract](https://epjds.epj.org/articles/epjdata/abs/2016/01/13688_2016_Article_93/13688_2016_Article_93.html); [arXiv 1606.07772](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772)
  - Discrepancy: a UVM page lists 1,737 books; the journal abstract says 1,327 (use 1,327) — [UVM ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.uvm.edu/cemsfac/26)
- Six arcs: "Rags to riches" (rise); "Tragedy"/"Riches to rags" (fall); "Man in a hole" (fall–rise); "Icarus" (rise–fall); "Cinderella" (rise–fall–rise); "Oedipus" (fall–rise–fall) — [Reagan et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07772)
- Most successful by downloads: "Icarus", "Oedipus", and two sequential "Man in a hole" arcs; authors caution results are influenced by individual high-download books — [Reagan et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07772)
- Best for: thinking about emotional pacing (video scripts, talks, ad spots, chapters); strength: simple, visual, empirically grounded; limitation: describes valence only, not causality/character; Gutenberg corpus = older, English, public-domain fiction. [Inference]

#### 7. Blake Snyder's Save the Cat! beat sheet (2005)
- Origin: Blake Snyder (1957–2009), *Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need* (2005); 15 beats from Opening Image to Final Image; also 10 genres defined by type of transformation (not setting) — [Save the Cat! official site](https://savethecat.com/get-started)
- 15 beats [list standard; partial confirmation in Fordham PDF / Writers Write]: Opening Image (p.1), Theme Stated (5), Set-Up (1–10), Catalyst (12), Debate (12–25), Break into Two (25), B Story (30), Fun and Games (30–55), Midpoint (55), Bad Guys Close In (55–75), All Is Lost (75), Dark Night of the Soul (75–85), Break into Three (85), Finale (85–110), Final Image (110) — [Writers Write: 15 beats](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1259963); page numbers ≈ one page per minute of screen time — [Nelson University: The Beat Sheet](https://www.nelson.edu/thoughthub/the-beat-sheet-skeleton-of-a-screenplay)
- Title meaning: hero does something likeable early (saves a cat) so audience roots for them. [BK]
- 10 genres: Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage, Buddy Love, Whydunit, The Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized, Superhero. [BK]
- Adapted for novels by Jessica Brody, *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel* (2018). [BK]
- Best for: commercial screenplays and genre novels; outlining; strength: very concrete, page-timed; limitation: widely blamed for homogenised, predictable Hollywood plots. [BK/Inference]

#### 8. Kishōtenketsu (起承転結, East Asian 4-part structure)
- Origin: from Chinese qǐ-chéng-zhuǎn-hé (起承轉合) used in four-line Chinese poetry (e.g., jueju); Fan Heng (1272–1330) described it as four styles of poetic writing; later shaped prose and essay forms; spread to Japan (kishōtenketsu) and Korea (gi-seung-jeon-gyeol) — [Wikipedia: Kishōtenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Four parts: Ki (introduction) – Shō (development, no major change) – Ten (twist, unexpected turn; the crux) – Ketsu (conclusion/reconciliation drawing the result) — [Wikipedia: Kishōtenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Used in yonkoma (4-panel manga); Nintendo designers Shigeru Miyamoto and Koichi Hayashida applied it to level design (*Super Mario Galaxy* 2007, *Super Mario 3D World* 2013) — [Wikipedia: Kishōtenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- "Without conflict" claim is contested: works based on it, e.g., *Parasite* (2019), can and often do contain conflict; the structure withholds a major element until part 3, often shifting genre — [Wikipedia: Kishōtenketsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Advocacy view: Western plot is a "face-off" whereas kishōtenketsu relies on exposition and contrast; the fourth act draws a conclusion from the contrast between two "straight" acts and a disconnected third — [Downes / "The significance of plot without conflict"](https://www.downes.ca/post/58577/rd) (advocacy piece, treat as one perspective)
- Example (classic yonkoma-style): 1 a girl waits at a bus stop; 2 it starts raining, she opens her umbrella; 3 (cut) a dog shakes itself off under a tree; 4 the girl shares her umbrella with the dog. [Illustrative, invented]
- Best for: comics, short video, slice-of-life, essays, poetry, product reveals with a twist; limitation: weaker for long sustained suspense; Western audiences may perceive "nothing happens". [Inference]

### Inferences
- The classic models nest: Aristotle (whole/causality) ⊃ Three-Act ⊃ Save the Cat (subdivides Act II); Campbell ⊃ Vogler (screen-adapted) ⊃ Harmon (compressed to 8). Vonnegut/Reagan are orthogonal — they measure emotional valence and can overlay any structural model.
- "Man in a hole" (fall–rise) ≈ the emotional shape of most hero's journey / three-act plots and of most marketing stories (problem → solution).
- Granularity is the main selection criterion: fewer steps (ABT, 4-act, 3-act) for short pieces; more steps (12 stages, 15 beats) for 90+ minutes or 60k+ words.

## Key Question 2: Business / communication frameworks

### Takeaway
Business frameworks strip story down to its minimal causal engine (status quo → disruption → resolution) and re-assign roles: the audience/customer becomes the hero (StoryBrand, Duarte), the communicator becomes the guide/mentor, and the "plot" becomes a problem→solution or argument. They trade narrative richness for speed, clarity and persuasion.

### Cited Findings

#### 9. Story Spine (Kenn Adams, late 1980s/c. 1991; used at Pixar)
- Creator: playwright/improviser Kenn Adams, artistic director of Synergy Theater (Walnut Creek, CA); author of *How to Improvise a Full-Length Play: The Art of Spontaneous Theater*. An 8-sentence structure where the sentence starters are fixed and the rest is improvised — [NPR, "Meet the creator of the Story Spine" (2026-06-23)](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619); syndicated e.g. [WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories)
- Date conflict: Adams says he began it in the late 1980s while with Freestyle Repertory Theatre in New York and running a middle-school creative-writing workshop that needed an outlining tool (per NPR); an improv blog says 1991 — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619); contradicted by [Sid Desai, "Improv Basics: Story Spine"](https://sidrdesai.substack.com/p/improv-basics-story-spine). Prefer Adams's own account.
- Path to Pixar: improv teacher Rebecca Stockley took Adams's workshop, later was hired to teach improvisation to Pixar's writers and taught the Story Spine there — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619). It is NOT a Pixar invention.
- Template (canonical form [BK for the full 8; Pixar rule #4 confirms 6]): Once upon a time… / Every day… / But one day… / Because of that… / Because of that… / Until finally… / And ever since then… / (The moral of the story is…). Pixar rule #4 version: "Once upon a time there was ___. Every day, ___. One day ___. Because of that, ___. Because of that, ___. Until finally ___." — [Gizmodo: The 22 rules of storytelling according to Pixar](https://gizmodo.com/the-22-rules-of-storytelling-according-to-pixar-5916970); [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/22-rules-pixar-uses-to-create-appealing-stories/)
- Best for: quickly drafting/testing any story, pitches, onboarding, kids, workshops; strength: forces causality ("because of that") and a status-quo break; limitation: linear, single-thread, little character interiority. [Inference]
- Example (Finding Nemo-style): Once upon a time there was an anxious clownfish, Marlin. Every day he overprotected his son Nemo. One day Nemo was captured by a diver. Because of that Marlin crossed the ocean. Because of that he learned to trust. Until finally he found Nemo and let him go to school. And ever since then… [Illustrative]

#### 10. ABT – And, But, Therefore (Randy Olson, 2015)
- Origin: Randy Olson, marine biologist turned filmmaker; *Houston, We Have a Narrative: Why Science Needs Story* (University of Chicago Press, 2015). Scientists' talks often read as "And, And, And" lists; narrative structure distilled to "And, But, Therefore": And = set-up/agreement & momentum, But = conflict/problem, Therefore = consequence/resolution — [Google Books: Houston, We Have a Narrative](https://books.google.com/books/about/Houston_We_Have_a_Narrative.html?id=qjhHCgAAQBAJ)
- Olson credits the idea to *South Park* creators Trey Parker & Matt Stone's "replace 'and' with 'but' or 'therefore'" rule; also defines "AAA" (boring) and "DHY" (Despite, However, Yet — confusing) templates and a "Narrative Spectrum"; the "Dobzhansky template" for one-word themes. [BK]
- Best for: abstracts, emails, elevator pitches, slide titles, research grants, opening of talks; 1–3 sentences. Strength: fastest test of whether a message has a narrative; limitation: no characters/emotion, just a logic skeleton. [Inference]
- Example: "Our team ships features fast AND customers love the product, BUT churn rose 20% because onboarding is confusing, THEREFORE we're rebuilding onboarding this quarter." [Illustrative]

#### 11. StoryBrand SB7 (Donald Miller, 2017)
- Origin: Donald Miller, *Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your Message So Customers Will Listen* (HarperCollins Leadership, 2017) — [Shortform: SB7 Formula](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=27392); [Graham Mann book notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/building-a-storybrand-donald-miller)
- 7 parts: A Character (customer) → Has a Problem (external, internal, philosophical; villain) → And Meets a Guide (brand: empathy + authority) → Who Gives Them a Plan → And Calls Them to Action → That Helps Them Avoid Failure → And Ends in a Success — [Shortform](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=27392); [StoryBrand.com](https://storybrand.com) [official site not fetched]
- Core inversion: customer is the hero, brand is the guide; slogan "If you confuse, you lose"; output is a "BrandScript" mapped onto website sections (e.g., hero section = character want + success + CTA) — [Graham Mann notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/building-a-storybrand-donald-miller); [Elementor: StoryBrand website examples](https://elementor.com/blog/storybrand-website-examples/)
- Derived from Hero's Journey / screenwriting (the guide = mentor archetype). [Inference]
- Best for: brand messaging, landing pages, sales emails, pitch one-liners; limitation: can make all brands sound alike; reduces story to conversion copy; commercial certification ecosystem. [Inference]
- Example (accounting SaaS): Small-business owners (character) drown in bookkeeping and fear tax mistakes (problem) → we've helped 10,000 firms (guide) → 3 steps: connect bank, auto-categorise, file (plan) → "Start free trial" (CTA) → avoid penalties (failure) → evenings back (success). [Illustrative]

#### 12. Nancy Duarte's Sparkline – "What is / What could be" (2010)
- Origin: Nancy Duarte, *Resonate: Present Visual Stories that Transform Audiences* (Wiley, 2010); TEDx talk "The secret structure of great talks" (2011) [BK for TEDx] — [Tom Tunguz review](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/); [Wikipedia: Nancy Duarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nancy_Duarte)
- Structure: great presentations oscillate between "what is" (status quo) and "what could be" (better future), creating tension; begins with what is, ends in "new bliss" (vision of the transformed future) plus call to action; shape is mapped as a "sparkline" (term borrowed from Edward Tufte's word-sized charts) — [Tom Tunguz](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/); [Architect Elevator: presenting waveform](https://architectelevator.com/strategy/presenting-waveform/)
- Turning points: "Call to adventure" (first turning point) and "Call to action" (second). [BK]
- Duarte analysed Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" and Steve Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch; also Reagan's Challenger address, Feynman, Benjamin Zander — [Tom Tunguz](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/) (Jobs/MLK examples: [BK] partly)
- Audience is the hero, presenter is the mentor (explicitly borrowed from Campbell). [BK]
- Best for: keynotes, change-management talks, investor pitches, all-hands. Strength: designed for persuasion of a live audience; limitation: needs real contrast & a credible future; less suited to purely informative briefings. [Inference]

#### 13. Minto Pyramid Principle & SCQA (Barbara Minto, McKinsey)
- Origin: Barbara Minto, joined McKinsey after Harvard Business School (1963), reportedly first female consultant; *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking* — [Think Insights: Pyramid Principle](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle); one source gives 1985 for the foundational book — [ModelThinkers](https://modelthinkers.com/mental-model/225)
- SCQA: Situation (context the audience already accepts) → Complication (change/challenge to status quo) → Question (that the complication raises) → Answer (your recommendation = top of pyramid). The Pyramid: answer/governing thought at top, key supporting arguments (MECE groups) in the middle, data at the bottom; SCQA forms the narrative introduction, the pyramid structures the logic beneath — [SlideModel: SCQA guide](https://slidemodel.com/scqa-framework-guide/); [Think Insights](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/pyramid-principle)
- Variants: answer-first ordering (A–S–C) for executives; SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution). [BK]
- Best for: consulting decks, executive memos, board papers, reports, emails to busy readers. Strength: answer-first, logically checkable; limitation: low emotional pull — it is "story" only in the S→C tension. [Inference]
- Example: S: We sell 70% via retail partners. C: Two largest partners are cutting shelf space next year. Q: How do we protect revenue? A: Launch DTC channel in Q1 — three reasons… [Illustrative]

#### 14. Before–After–Bridge (BAB) — copywriting formula
- Structure: Before (reader's current painful world) → After (world once the problem is solved) → Bridge (how to get there = your product). Commonly taught for emails, ads, social posts. [BK]
- No reliably documented originator found (see Gaps). Best for: short sales copy, social posts, case-study teasers; maps exactly onto Duarte's what is / what could be compressed into one pass. [Inference]
- Example: "Before: 3 hours a day answering the same support questions. After: customers find answers themselves and your team handles only the hard tickets. Bridge: [Tool] builds a help centre from your past tickets in 10 minutes." [Illustrative]

#### 15. PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve (Solution)
- Structure: name the Problem → Agitate (intensify the pain/consequences, emotional stakes) → Solve (present solution). Classic direct-response formula; often attributed loosely to direct-response copywriters (e.g., Dan Kennedy calls it the most reliable formula) — attribution unverified. [BK]
- Best for: ads, landing pages, cold emails, short-form video hooks. Strength: emotionally strong, very short; limitation: can feel manipulative/fear-based; overused. [Inference]

#### 16. STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result (behavioural interviews)
- Grew out of behavioural interviewing (premise: past behaviour predicts future performance); Development Dimensions International (DDI) is closely tied — its "Targeted Selection" system; exact formaliser/date of STAR is undocumented — [ASEE paper "Helping Students Become Interview STARs" (2005)](https://peer.asee.org/helping-students-become-interview-stars); [Sonovate blog](https://www.sonovate.com/?p=9566)
- A 1974 DDI date circulates but comes from an AI-generated wiki and is unverified — [Grokipedia](https://grokipedia.com/page/Situation,_task,_action,_result) (low reliability)
- Variants: STAR-L / STARR (add Learning/Reflection), CAR (Challenge-Action-Result), SOAR. [BK]
- Best for: interviews, performance reviews, résumé bullets, case studies. Strength: concise, verifiable evidence; limitation: can be flat — best answers add stakes/conflict (the "Task" ≈ complication) and a reflection. [Inference]
- Example: S: our checkout conversion fell 12% after a redesign. T: I owned the recovery within one sprint. A: ran session-replay analysis, found a hidden coupon field, A/B tested fix. R: conversion +15% over baseline within two weeks; learned to ship with funnel alerts. [Illustrative]

#### 17. Pixar's 22 Rules of Storytelling (Emma Coats, 2011)
- Origin: Emma Coats, then a Pixar storyboard artist, tweeted 22 "story basics" she'd learned from colleagues over about two months in 2011; widely republished in 2012 as "Pixar's 22 Rules". They are a personal list, not an official Pixar doctrine — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-22-rules-of-storytelling-according-to-pixar-5916970); [Tubefilter (2012-06-12)](https://tubefilter.com/2012/06/12/pixar-rules-of-storytelling/); [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/22-rules-pixar-uses-to-create-appealing-stories/)
- Rule #4 is the Story Spine template ("Once upon a time there was ___…"), which originated with Kenn Adams, not Pixar — [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/the-22-rules-of-storytelling-according-to-pixar-5916970); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5750619)
- Other well-known rules [BK, check against Farnam Street/Gizmodo text]: #1 You admire a character for trying more than for their successes; #2 keep in mind what's interesting to you as an audience, not what's fun to do as a writer; #6 What is your character good at/comfortable with? Throw the polar opposite at them; #7 Come up with your ending before your middle; #9 When stuck, list what wouldn't happen next; #14 Why must you tell THIS story?; #16 What are the stakes? Stack odds against; #19 Coincidences to get characters into trouble are great; to get them out are cheating; #22 What's the essence of your story? Most economical telling?
- Best for: a checklist/heuristics companion to any structure, not a structure itself. [Inference]

### Inferences
- Shared skeleton: Story Spine "every day / but one day / because of that / until finally" ≈ ABT ≈ SCQA (S/C/Q-A) ≈ BAB (before/after/bridge) ≈ PAS ≈ STAR (S-T/A-R). All encode status quo → disruption → resolution.
- Role reassignment is the key innovation of SB7 and Duarte: the audience is the hero; the speaker/brand is the mentor (Vogler's "Meeting with the Mentor").
- Emotional vs logical axis: PAS/BAB/SB7/Duarte lean emotional-persuasive; SCQA/Minto/STAR lean logical-evidential; ABT and Story Spine sit in between.

### Gaps
- No reliable primary origin for BAB or PAS (both are folk copywriting formulas; attributions to specific copywriters were not verified).
- Exact first-publication year of Minto's *Pyramid Principle*: sources give 1985 (ModelThinkers); other common claims (1973 McKinsey training material, 1978 Minto International edition, 1987 Pitman) were not verified.
- StoryBrand.com and Duarte.com primary pages were not successfully fetched (Duarte URL tried returned 404); descriptions come from reviews/summaries.
- Olson's attribution of ABT to Parker & Stone and his DHY/AAA terms are [BK], unverified this session.

## Key Question 3: Which frameworks fit which contexts?

### Takeaway
Pick by length and goal: 1–3 sentences/logic → ABT, SCQA; short persuasive copy → PAS, BAB; brand/website → StoryBrand SB7; live talks → Duarte sparkline (+ SCQA for executive audiences); interviews/reviews → STAR; short video/episodes → Story Circle, Story Spine, kishōtenketsu; films/novels → Three-Act, Save the Cat, Hero's Journey/Vogler; emotional pacing anywhere → Vonnegut/Reagan arcs.

### Cited Findings
- ABT is pitched as applicable "far beyond science" and as a tool for talks/abstracts — [Google Books: Houston, We Have a Narrative](https://books.google.com/books/about/Houston_We_Have_a_Narrative.html?id=qjhHCgAAQBAJ)
- SCQA is framed for structured business communication and paired with the Pyramid for decks/reports — [SlideModel](https://slidemodel.com/scqa-framework-guide/)
- SB7 maps directly onto website sections (BrandScript) — [Elementor](https://elementor.com/blog/storybrand-website-examples/)
- Duarte's sparkline is derived from analysis of great speeches/presentations — [Tom Tunguz](https://tomtunguz.com/storytelling-duarte/)
- STAR is the standard answer format for behavioural interview questions — [ASEE paper](https://peer.asee.org/helping-students-become-interview-stars)
- Save the Cat's page-timed beats target ~110-page feature screenplays — [Nelson University](https://www.nelson.edu/thoughthub/the-beat-sheet-skeleton-of-a-screenplay); [Save the Cat!](https://savethecat.com/get-started)
- Kishōtenketsu is used in 4-panel manga and Nintendo level design — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu)
- Story Circle presented as general-purpose, descriptive — [Spines](https://spines.com/dan-harmon-story-circle/)

### Inferences
- Length heuristic: number of steps should scale with duration — ~3 steps for <1 min, 4–8 for 1–20 min, 12–15 for feature length.
- Combining is normal: e.g., talk = Duarte macro-structure + ABT per section + Story Spine anecdotes; landing page = SB7 overall + PAS in hero copy; screenplay = Three-Act + Save the Cat beats + Hero's Journey for character arc.

### Gaps
- No empirical comparative study found showing one business framework outperforms another; fit recommendations are practitioner consensus/inference.

## Key Question 4: Common critiques

### Takeaway
The biggest critiques: (a) Hero's Journey is Western-/male-centric, built on selective evidence, and becomes formulaic when used as a template; (b) beat-sheet/three-act formulas homogenise commercial film; (c) "conflict is essential" is a culturally specific assumption, though "kishōtenketsu = no conflict" is itself an oversimplification; (d) business formulas oversimplify and can manipulate.

### Cited Findings
- Hero's Journey — critics say Campbell overgeneralises across cultures, imposing a Western, patriarchal framework; the pattern centres a male protagonist with women as goddess/temptress/prize; Maureen Murdock's "Heroine's Journey" (1990) offered as a feminist alternative emphasising relationship and inner transformation — [RemNote study deck: Hero's journey critique](https://www.remnote.com/learn/literature/folklore-and-mythology/heros-journey-critique-and-extended-study-study-deck) (study aid, secondary); Murdock date [BK]
- Maria Tatar observed Campbell places women at the destination, limiting them to domestic space — [UNBC master's thesis](https://unbc.arcabc.ca/_flysystem/repo-bin/2022-09/unbc_59315.pdf)
- Folklorists: Barre Toelken argued Campbell chose myths that fit his predetermined pattern (circular reasoning); Alan Dundes called Campbell a "non-expert" relying on biased secondhand sources — [RemNote study deck](https://www.remnote.com/learn/literature/folklore-and-mythology/heros-journey-critique-and-extended-study-study-deck) (secondary; original critiques not fetched)
- The modern hero is "profoundly individualistic", tied to mid-20th-c. American culture; the book was written when attitudes to masculinity, conflict and non-Western cultures were far from today's — [Big Think: monomyth](https://bigthink.com/high-culture/monomyth-heros-journey-campbell/); [Sharon Blackie, "The post-heroic journey"](https://sharonblackie.substack.com/p/the-post-heroic-journey)
- Monomyth "can become formulaic if applied too rigidly" — [RemNote](https://www.remnote.com/learn/literature/folklore-and-mythology/heros-journey-critique-and-extended-study-study-deck)
- Matthew Salesses, *Craft in the Real World* (2021), argues not all traditions favour conflict or character-driven models like the hero's journey; Western workshops treat "nothing happens" as a flaw — the same complaint readers make of Japanese fiction — [Touring a World of Story Structures (Substack)](https://jmguilfoyle.substack.com/p/touring-a-world-of-story-structures); [The Millions: On East Asian Narratives](https://themillions.com/?p=102455)
- Kishōtenketsu "without conflict" label is contested — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu); see also [Language Hat discussion](https://languagehat.com/kishotenketsu/)
- Three-act: James Bonnet "What's Wrong With The Three Act Structure" vs Yves Lavandier's defence — [Wikipedia: Three-act structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-act_structure)
- Freytag: hard to fit modern plays — [Scribophile](https://www.scribophile.com/blog/freytags-pyramid/)
- Reagan et al.: popularity results skewed by individual high-download books — [Reagan et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.07772)
- Save the Cat: commonly blamed (e.g., Peter Suderman, Slate 2013, "Save the Movie!") for making Hollywood blockbusters formulaic/identical. [BK — not fetched]
- Story Circle presented by proponents as descriptive not prescriptive (a defence against the "formula" critique) — [Spines](https://spines.com/dan-harmon-story-circle/)

### Inferences
- Common defence across Campbell, Vogler, Harmon, Snyder: they are descriptive diagnostic tools for revision, not fill-in templates; most critiques target mechanical use rather than the models themselves.
- Business frameworks inherit the critique in a new form: SB7/PAS make messages sound alike and can drift into fear-based manipulation; SCQA/STAR can be dry. Mitigation: use specific, true details and real stakes.
- For a Vietnamese/East Asian audience, kishōtenketsu (and the Chinese/Vietnamese essay tradition "khai–thừa–chuyển–hợp", the Sino-Vietnamese reading of 起承轉合) may feel more native than Western conflict-driven arcs. [Inference; Vietnamese term from Sino-Vietnamese reading — BK]

### Gaps
- Primary texts of Toelken, Dundes, Tatar critiques not fetched.
- No academic source fetched on critiques of StoryBrand/PAS/BAB specifically; critiques listed are inference.
- Slate "Save the Movie!" article not verified this session.
